research

Learning reliable structure from noisy, heterogeneous, real-world signals.

A common thread in my work is that real-world data rarely looks like benchmark data. Speech is accented and code-switched, sensors drift and fail, and models trained in one setting quietly break in another. I am interested in methods, and in evaluations, that make these failure modes visible and fixable. My background in signal processing and stochastic processes shapes how I approach these problems: I treat the data-generating process as a first-class object and not just a source of training examples.

In African speech this matters directly. Many models look strong on paper and fail in deployment because the conditions that break them (code-switching within a sentence, numbers and named entities, specialised vocabulary in health, law and finance, noisy phone audio) were rarely measured. Collecting more of the same data, or generating synthetic speech without first characterising these failures, has not closed the gap. My work starts by making the failures visible, then targets them.

current directions

Speech recognition for African languages

Benchmarks and systems for accented, multilingual, and code-switched speech in the wild. I led the AfriSpeech-MultiBench (100+ accents, 10+ countries, seven domains) and AfriSwitch benchmarks; AfriSwitch uses real, spontaneous code-switched speech annotated at each switch, not synthetic code-switched sentences. I also contributed to AfriVox-v2. To understand failures, not just count them, I work with native-speaker reviewers in more than 30 languages. These studies show that open-source ASR degrades on noisy, non-native dialogue, multimodal LLMs struggle with domain-specific named entities, and hallucination remains common. At Intron Health I benchmarked 19 ASR models, built a production African-accented English system that outperforms them, and am building a multilingual ASR system covering 15 African languages spoken by about 90% of speakers on the continent.

Reliable large language models

Quantifying and mitigating selection bias in multiple-choice evaluation through LoRA fine-tuning and efficient majority voting, and adapting LLMs to specialised domains such as telecommunications (QMOS).

LLMs · evaluation · bias · domain adaptation

Robust computer vision

Unified detection of physical and digital face attacks with triplet embeddings and a differentiable Soft-ACER loss (TSANet), and large-scale OCR data for under-resourced scripts (Fidel, Amharic).

anti-spoofing · metric learning · OCR

Time-series & sensor data

Forecasting and anomaly detection for CO₂/O₂ telemetry in cold-storage IoT systems, and sensor fusion under uncertainty. This is where my interest in learning from noisy observational signals started.

looking ahead: research agenda

My benchmark work keeps showing the same pattern: speech models that look strong on standard test sets degrade sharply on accented, code-switched, and domain-specific speech, and we have few tools to predict or repair that failure. Next, I want to move from measuring these failures to fixing them. I am interested in how self-supervised and multilingual speech representations break under accent, domain, and language shift; how to adapt them to new varieties with very little labeled data; and how to give ASR systems calibrated uncertainty so they know when they are likely to be wrong. I want to approach these questions with the same signal-processing and probabilistic lens that runs through the rest of my work, and with under-resourced languages as a primary testbed rather than an afterthought.

The same questions apply to multilingual large language models. In my benchmarks, multimodal LLMs struggle with domain-specific named entities and hallucinate on African speech, and my work on LLM selection bias showed how easily evaluation can mislead. I am interested in how multilingual LLMs and speech-language models represent under-resourced languages, how to extend them to new languages without degrading the ones they already cover, and how to evaluate them in ways that reflect how speakers actually use those languages, including code-switching.

Concretely, questions I would like to work on:

  • Why do pretrained speech representations degrade on accented and code-switched speech, and can we predict that degradation before deployment?
  • How can a multilingual ASR model be adapted to a new language or accent from a few hours of labeled audio without forgetting the others?
  • How should code-switching be modeled inside the recognizer rather than only addressed through more data?
  • Can ASR systems produce calibrated, word-level confidence that flags hallucinations and domain errors (names, numbers, medical terms)?
  • How can multilingual LLMs be adapted to a low-resource language, in text and speech, without forgetting the others, and how do we measure whether they actually work for its speakers?
